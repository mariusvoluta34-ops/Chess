♟️ C++ Chess Game — Project Guide
Phase 1 — Decide the architecture

Before coding, decide what your major objects are.

A reasonable starting architecture:

ChessGame
    │
    ├── Board
    │     └── 8 × 8 squares
    │
    ├── Pieces
    │     ├── Pawn
    │     ├── Rook
    │     ├── Knight
    │     ├── Bishop
    │     ├── Queen
    │     └── King
    │
    └── Player

You don't necessarily need exactly these classes. The point is to separate game logic from individual pieces.

For example, don't make the Queen class responsible for deciding whether the entire game is over.

Phase 2 — Represent the board

Start with an 8×8 board.

Think about how you want to represent a square:

a8 b8 c8 d8 e8 f8 g8 h8
a7 b7 c7 d7 e7 f7 g7 h7
...
a1 b1 c1 d1 e1 f1 g1 h1

You need to decide:

How are coordinates represented?
How do you represent an empty square?
How do you identify piece color?
How do you identify piece type?

You could use:

Piece
 ├── color
 └── type

or inheritance:

Piece
 ├── Pawn
 ├── Rook
 ├── Knight
 ├── Bishop
 ├── Queen
 └── King

I'd recommend experimenting with both approaches before settling.

Phase 3 — Implement the pieces

Get all six pieces represented.

Each piece should at minimum know:

Color
Position
Type

Then you need to determine how each piece moves.

Don't worry about check/checkmate yet.

Implement movement independently:

Pawn
White:
  ↑
  ↑
  ↑

Special cases:

One square forward
Two squares from starting position
Diagonal capture
Promotion
Rook
← ← ←
  ↑
  ↑
  ↓
→ → →
Bishop

Diagonal movement.

Knight

The weird one:

  X X
X     X
X     X
  X X

It can jump over pieces.

Queen

Rook + bishop.

King

One square in any direction.

Phase 4 — Collision and blocking

Now make movement interact with the board.

For sliding pieces:

Rook → → → → 

you need to stop when encountering another piece.

For example:

R R R . . . R

The rook cannot move through the piece.

You need to distinguish:

empty square
friendly piece
enemy piece

This is where your board logic starts becoming important.

Phase 5 — Implement moves

Create the concept of a move.

A move should conceptually contain something like:

starting square
destination square
piece
captured piece

Eventually you'll want additional information:

promotion
castling
en passant

Don't add all of that immediately.

First make:

Select piece → select destination → perform move.

Phase 6 — Turn system

Implement:

White's turn
    ↓
White makes move
    ↓
Black's turn
    ↓
Black makes move
    ↓
White's turn

You need to prevent:

Moving an opponent's piece
Moving a piece that can't move there
Moving outside the board

At this point you should have a very ugly but functional chess game.

That's good.

Phase 7 — Capturing

When a piece moves onto an enemy piece:

Before:

White Queen → Black Pawn


After:

White Queen

Remove the captured piece from the board.

Don't actually destroy objects unnecessarily if your architecture doesn't require it. Think carefully about ownership and lifetime of pieces.

This is a good point to practice proper C++ memory management.

Phase 8 — Check

This is where the project gets significantly more interesting.

You need a function/concept equivalent to:

"Is this king currently attacked?"

Don't think about check as a special move.

Instead:

Is square X attacked by any enemy piece?

Then:

Is my king on an attacked square?

That's check.

This abstraction will become extremely useful later.

Phase 9 — Legal moves

This is one of the most important parts of the project.

A piece might theoretically be able to move somewhere, but the move can still be illegal.

Example:

White King
    ↓
White Rook

Black Rook attacks the king

If White moves the rook away, the king becomes exposed.

Therefore:

A move is legal only if it doesn't leave your own king in check.

A very useful conceptual algorithm is:

Generate possible move
        ↓
Temporarily make move
        ↓
Is my king attacked?
       / \
     YES  NO
      ↓    ↓
   illegal legal

This concept is extremely important if you later make a chess AI.

Phase 10 — Checkmate

Now you can define checkmate elegantly.

King is in check
        +
There are ZERO legal moves
        ↓
     CHECKMATE

Don't create a giant collection of special cases.

Think in terms of:

Is player in check?

Does player have ANY legal move?

If:

check == true
AND
legalMoves.empty()

→ checkmate.

Phase 11 — Stalemate

Almost the same idea:

King NOT in check
        +
ZERO legal moves
        ↓
     STALEMATE

This is a good example of why designing your move system properly matters.

Phase 12 — Special chess rules

Once normal chess works, implement the special rules.

Castling

Track whether:

King has moved
Relevant rook has moved
Squares between them are empty
King isn't currently in check
King doesn't cross an attacked square
King doesn't end in check
En passant

This is probably the most annoying rule to implement.

You'll need to remember information about the previous move.

For example:

Previous move:
Pawn moved two squares

That information can make an en-passant capture available for the next move.

Promotion

When:

Pawn reaches opposite end

allow:

Queen
Rook
Bishop
Knight

Don't automatically assume queen unless that's what you want for the first version.

Phase 13 — Move history

Now start storing every move.

Something like:

Move 1
Move 2
Move 3
...

This gives you:

Undo
Redo
Move display
Saving games
Replaying games

And you'll learn a lot about copying/restoring game state.

Phase 14 — Save/load games

Create your own file format initially.

For example conceptually:

Player: White
Turn: Black

Board:
rnbqkbnr
pppppppp
........
........
........
........
PPPPPPPP
RNBQKBNR

Then save:

game.chess

Later you can implement FEN and PGN if you want to make the project more serious.

Phase 15 — Make an actual UI

Only after the underlying chess logic works.

You could use:

SFML
SDL
raylib

Your UI should basically communicate with your chess engine:

          UI
           ↓
       ChessGame
           ↓
         Board
           ↓
        Pieces

The UI should not contain your chess rules.

For example, clicking a square should tell the game:

"The user wants to move from e2 to e4."

The chess logic decides whether that's legal.

This separation will save you a massive headache later.

Phase 16 — Add game features

Once you have a complete two-player chess game, start adding quality-of-life features:

Highlight legal moves
Highlight last move
Highlight check
Capture animations
Piece animations
Chess clock
Move list
Resign
Draw
Threefold repetition
50-move rule
Insufficient material
Undo
Redo
Save/load

At this point you have a proper chess application.

Phase 17 — Make the AI

This is where I'd recommend basically starting a second project inside the project.

First create:

Human vs Human

Then:

Human vs Random AI

The random AI simply:

Generate legal moves
        ↓
Choose random move
        ↓
Play it

Then improve it.

AI Level 1 — Material evaluation

Give pieces values:

Pawn     1
Knight   3
Bishop   3
Rook     5
Queen    9
King    ∞

Then evaluate:

White material - Black material

Now the AI can at least understand:

"Capturing a queen is probably good."

AI Level 2 — Minimax

Now make the AI look ahead.

Conceptually:

             Current position
              /            \
          Move A          Move B
          /   \            /   \
        ...   ...        ...   ...

The AI explores possible futures.

White tries to maximize the evaluation.

Black tries to minimize it.

That's minimax.

AI Level 3 — Alpha-beta pruning

Your minimax tree will become enormous.

So implement:

alpha-beta pruning

This lets you avoid evaluating branches that cannot possibly affect the final decision.

This is where your algorithmic skills will really get tested.

AI Level 4 — Better evaluation

Instead of only:

material

consider:

Material
+
Piece-square tables
+
King safety
+
Pawn structure
+
Mobility
+
Center control
+
Passed pawns

Now you have an actual chess engine.

AI Level 5 — Transposition table

This is where your knowledge of data structures becomes very useful.

Different move sequences can lead to the same position.

Instead of evaluating that position repeatedly:

Position X
   ↓
Already evaluated?
   ↓
YES → reuse result

Use a hash table keyed by a representation of the board.

Eventually you can learn about Zobrist hashing, which is commonly used for chess positions.
